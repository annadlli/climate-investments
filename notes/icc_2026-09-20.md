# Increased Cost of Compliance (ICC) and the harmonized elevation variable

Claude, 2026-09-20, for issue #25 (Vendela 09-15: document what ICC means and adjust the
harmonized elevation variable for buyouts). Data appendix material; verify the program facts
against the current NFIP Flood Insurance Manual (ICC section) before they go in the paper.

## What ICC is

- ICC is Coverage D of the Standard Flood Insurance Policy. It pays, on top of the building
  claim, for the cost of bringing a flood-damaged building into compliance with the community's
  floodplain management ordinance. Limit $30,000 per policy (nominal, unchanged since 2003; the
  earlier limit was $20,000; coverage began in 1997). Building and ICC together cannot exceed the
  $250,000 residential building limit.
- Trigger: the community's floodplain administrator declares the building substantially damaged
  (repair cost at least 50 percent of pre-flood market value) or, in communities whose ordinance
  has the provision, repetitively damaged (two floods in ten years, each at least 25 percent).
  ICC is not a policyholder choice; without the community determination there is no ICC claim.
- Eligible measures: elevation, relocation, demolition, and floodproofing (floodproofing for
  non-residential buildings only). Any one, or a combination (e.g. demolition and rebuild elevated).
- ICC is paid when the mitigation is complete and inspected, so the payment year lags the flood
  year. In the OpenFEMA claims file the ICC amount (`netIccPaymentAmount`) sits on the claim record
  of the flood that triggered it, dated by `yearOfLoss`, not by the year the work was done.
- ICC money can be combined with FEMA mitigation grants: it can serve as the non-federal share of
  an HMGP or FMA elevation, and it can pay for the demolition of a structure that is then acquired
  under a buyout. So an ICC payment is evidence of one of four outcomes, two of which (demolition,
  relocation) remove the house.

## How the panel uses it

- `clean_nfip_claims.do`: `claim_icc` = net ICC payment, 2023 dollars, summed within property-year.
- `merge_nfip_hma.do`: `nfip_icc` = first policy year with `claim_icc > 0` (the flood year).
- `complete.do`: an ICC payment is an elevation event (`elevation_source` 2) only if the property
  has no Builty permit and no NFIP flag flip, and only if it stays on the panel after the ICC
  year (`nfip_icc < _last_year`). The continued-coverage rule is the buyout screen: a demolished
  or acquired house does not renew its policy.

## Test of the screen (NFIP panel, FL LA TX, 09-20)

2,618 properties with an ICC payment (FL 900, LA 903, TX 815). Median payment $36.4k in 2023
dollars, 90th percentile $39.8k, i.e. the $30k cap deflated; one property at $1.0m is an id
collision.

| | Stay on panel after ICC year | Leave in ICC year |
|---|---|---|
| Share of ICC properties | 57% | 43% |
| Already flagged elevated at first observation | 48% | 22% |
| NFIP elevated flag flips 0 to 1 at or after ICC year | 0.7% | 0% |
| Elevated in last observed year | 50% | 22% |
| HMA acquisition project in the county within 3 years | 70% | 60% |

By state: stayers FL 50%, LA 65%, TX 55%. Acquisition exposure by county is near universal in
LA (83-88%) and TX (80-84%) and does not separate stayers from leavers; FL 27-32%.

What this says:

1. The continued-coverage rule removes 43% of ICC properties. That share is consistent with
   demolition and relocation being common ICC outcomes, but leaving the panel also happens for
   ordinary lapse, so the rule over-screens by an unknown amount. County-level HMA acquisition
   exposure cannot sharpen it; a property-level buyout list would (HMA FOIA property lists, or
   ATTOM: a sale to a public entity or a parcel that becomes vacant after the ICC year).
2. Among stayers the NFIP elevated flag almost never flips after the ICC payment (0.7%). Half of
   the stayers were already flagged elevated before the flood, and the other half (760 properties)
   never show as elevated. So the NFIP flag does not corroborate ICC-funded elevations. Either the
   flag is not updated after mitigation (it comes from the elevation certificate at rating and
   many policies are not re-rated), or the ICC money went to demolition-and-rebuild or relocation
   within the same block group and construction cohort, which keeps the property id.
3. The 48% already elevated is itself a puzzle: an elevated house being substantially damaged
   and then brought into compliance again is possible but should be rare. Worth checking whether
   `elevated` in the policy file is constant within property over years (a current-rating field
   carried back) rather than a per-year characteristic.

## Recommendation for the harmonized variable

- Keep ICC as a source, but split it: `elevation_source` 2 = ICC with continued coverage (as now),
  and report in the appendix that the NFIP flag corroborates almost none of these. Do not treat
  the ICC-only group as verified elevations in the event studies; the Builty-permit treated group
  is the defensible one, which is what `es_elevation.do` already does.
- Buyouts: the continued-coverage rule is the only screen available now. Replace it with a
  property-level one when the LA GOHSEP list (due end October 2026) and the RI records arrive;
  in the meantime, a cheap ATTOM check is whether the assigned ATTOM property records a transfer
  to a government grantee or a demolition permit in the two years after the ICC year.
- The `elevated`-constant-within-property question (point 3) should be answered before the
  NFIP flag flip (`elevation_source` 1) is trusted either; if the flag is a current-rating field,
  flips are re-ratings, not elevations.

## Addendum: the NFIP elevated flag (panel check, 09-20)

- `clean_nfip_policies.do` forces `elevated` to be monotone within property (line 76: running
  max over policy years), so 1 to 0 changes never appear by construction; the raw
  `elevatedBuildingIndicator` does vary and that variation is not observable in the panel.
- Of 5.38M properties with more than one policy year, 2,238 (0.04%) ever flip 0 to 1. 711 of the
  flips are in 2010, the first transition year of the panel, which looks like a start-of-panel
  artifact rather than 711 elevations; 2016-2017 (576) line up with the 2016 Louisiana floods
  and Harvey. After 2021 flips almost stop (58, 25, 1), while the share of property-years flagged
  elevated falls from 10% to 6.5% between 2021 and 2025: Risk Rating 2.0 changed the rating
  inputs and the indicator is populated differently after it.
- So the NFIP flag flip is rare, front-loaded and dries up under Risk Rating 2.0. Before the
  flips (`elevation_source` 1) are used as events, check the raw indicator in the per-state
  clean files: how often it goes 1 to 0, and whether post-2021 records carry it at all. If the
  2010 spike is the first year the field is reliably filled, drop 2010 flips.

## Addendum 2: the raw indicator (per-state raw policy files, 09-20)

Property x policy year, `elevatedBuildingIndicator` before the monotone cleaning:

| State | Properties | 0 to 1 transitions | 1 to 0 transitions | 1 to 0 in 2022-2025 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FL | 4.33M | 752 | 3,176 | 1,856 |
| LA | 1.46M | 855 | 1,601 | 911 |
| TX | 2.35M | 621 | 893 | 570 |

The raw flag is removed twice as often as it is added, and 60 percent of the removals fall in
the Risk Rating 2.0 years, when the rating inputs were re-entered. The 0 to 1 transitions are
the same 2,228 the panel keeps (2010 spike included: FL 278, LA 331, TX 90). Almost no property
shows both directions (79 in all), so the additions and removals are different homes: the
additions are plausibly real (2016-2017 LA and TX floods stand out), the removals are
re-ratings. Conclusion: the monotone cleaning is the right call for the flag as a stock, but a
flip is a rare event (0.04 percent of homes) whose 2010 share is a panel-start artifact. Drop
2010 flips from `elevation_source` 1, or require the flip to persist and to follow a claim.

## Addendum 3: buyout screens beyond continued coverage (09-20, Anna's deck note: "NPR buyouts, continued presence in ATTOM and NFIP")

Two more screens tested on the same 2,618 ICC properties.

**ZIP-level funded acquisitions.** OpenFEMA's mitigated-properties file (the file behind NPR's
buyout series) has 6,206 acquisition property records in FL, LA and TX, 5,551 of them under
closed, approved or obligated projects, with a ZIP on 99.97 percent. Share of ICC properties
with a funded acquisition in their own ZIP within three years of the ICC year: stayers 37%,
leavers 37% (FL 12 vs 8, LA 39 vs 43, TX 59 vs 66). Any acquisition in the ZIP ever: 55% both.
Buyout proximity does not separate the homes that drop coverage from those that keep it.

**ATTOM record after the ICC year** (assigned ATTOM property, tax years ICC+1 to ICC+4):

| | FL stayers | FL leavers | LA stayers | LA leavers | TX stayers | TX leavers |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Linked ICC properties | 405 | 387 | 259 | 68 | 387 | 301 |
| No ATTOM record after | 24% | 30% | 2% | 0% | 1% | 1% |
| Improvement value falls to 0 | 3.5% | 5.4% | 3.9% | 4.4% | 3.9% | 3.7% |
| Any sale after | 21% | 19% | 21% | 19% | 1% | 0% |
| Sale after under $20k | 1.7% | 1.0% | 3.1% | 2.9% | 0% | 0% |

Only about 4 percent of the linked properties show a demolition signature (improvements to
zero), the same for stayers and leavers, and nominal transfers are 0-3 percent. The FL
"no record after" rate is the FL ATTOM file's year coverage, not demolition (it is the same
for both groups). Two caveats make this a weak test: the ATTOM property is the cell-level
proxy, not the ICC house, and the ATTOM history ends in 2024.

**Conclusion.** Nothing observable distinguishes the 43 percent of ICC properties that leave
the panel from the 57 percent that stay: not buyout activity in the ZIP, not the ATTOM
record, not the NFIP flag. The continued-coverage rule stays as the only screen, unvalidated.
A property-level buyout list (GOHSEP, RI, or the FEMA GO site inventories for FY2020+) is the
only way to close this; until then ICC-sourced elevations should be reported as
uncorroborated, and the event studies should keep using Builty permits only.

## Addendum 4: NPR buyout list (09-21)

`~/Downloads/fema npr.csv` (NPR's FEMA buyout table, 41,004 address-level acquisitions, fiscal
years 1989-2017; the archived `clean_npr.do` read the same file in May). FL 542, LA 762, TX
3,706 records, 4,737 with a usable address and ZIP. Matched to ATTOM addresses with the Builty
rungs (exact normalized address + ZIP, then house number + first street word + ZIP, unique in
the ZIP): FL 277 of 542, LA 398 of 762, TX 2,443 of 3,706, 62 percent overall. Saved as
`build/npr_buyouts_attom.parquet` (data, on Dropbox).

Carried through the ATTOM-to-NFIP links, 1,677 NFIP panel properties sit on a bought-out
address (FL 167, LA 140, TX 1,370). But their last policy year is a median 12 years after the
buyout fiscal year, and only 7.5 percent leave the panel within a year of it. A bought-out house
does not renew for a decade, so these NFIP properties are not the bought-out houses: the
ATTOM-to-NFIP assignment is the cell-level proxy (block group x construction cohort), and the
NFIP id survives on the other houses in the cell. The same limitation as addendum 3.

Against the ICC set: 2 of 2,618 ICC properties match a buyout address (one stayer, one leaver).
The NPR file ends in fiscal 2017, so it can only ever cover ICC payments from 2009-2017.

Conclusion: the NPR list is usable at the ATTOM level (a bought-out ATTOM property is a real
event) but cannot tag NFIP panel properties, so it cannot screen ICC buyouts on this panel. It
would if the NFIP-ATTOM link were address-level, which the redacted policy file does not allow.

## Data appendix paragraph (final form, 09-21; sources below)

Increased Cost of Compliance (ICC) is Coverage D of the NFIP Standard Flood Insurance Policy.
Created by the National Flood Insurance Reform Act of 1994 and included in nearly every policy
since June 1997 (not contents-only, group, or Emergency Program policies), it pays, on top of
the building claim, the cost of bringing a flood-damaged building into compliance with the
community's floodplain management ordinance. The limit is $30,000 per policy ($15,000 in
1997-1999, $20,000 in 2000-2002), and building claim plus ICC cannot exceed the $250,000
residential building limit. ICC is triggered by a community determination that the building is
substantially damaged (repair cost at least 50 percent of pre-flood market value) or, where the
ordinance has the provision, repetitively damaged (two floods in ten years, each at least 25
percent of value, both with paid claims); in practice 99 percent of ICC claims are substantial
damage determinations. Eligible measures are elevation, relocation, demolition, or a
combination; floodproofing qualifies only for non-residential buildings. The policyholder has
six years from the date of loss to complete the work (four years before 2015), payment follows
completion and inspection, with up to half advanced on a signed contract, and the claim is
filed separately from the building claim. ICC money can serve as the non-federal share of a
FEMA mitigation grant and can pay for the demolition of a house that is then acquired.
Nationally, 1997-2014, ICC paid about 27,000 single-family claims worth $0.7 billion (2016
dollars), an average of $25,000 with half at the cap; 62 percent of the money went to
elevation, 30 percent to demolition, under 1 percent to relocation and 8 percent to other
measures; 86 percent of recipients were pre-FIRM buildings and 92 percent were in the SFHA;
Louisiana, at $273 million, is the largest state. In the OpenFEMA claims file the ICC amount
(`netIccPaymentAmount`) sits on the record of the triggering flood, dated by `yearOfLoss`,
so the panel's ICC year is the flood year, not the year the work was done.

How the panel uses it. An ICC payment counts as an elevation only for properties with no
Builty permit and no NFIP flag flip, and only if the property keeps its policy after the ICC
year, since demolition and relocation end coverage. On the SFHA analysis sample 2,183 homes
received an ICC payment (FL 790, LA 751, TX 642); 1,259 enter the harmonized variable as
ICC-sourced elevations (FL 405, LA 506, TX 349), 37 are overridden by a permit or a flag flip,
and 887 (41 percent) are screened out because coverage ends in the ICC year. The screened
share is in line with the national split of ICC money between demolition and elevation (30
versus 62 percent) once ordinary policy lapse is added, so the rule removes roughly the right
number, but it cannot tell a demolished house from a lapsed policy on any individual property,
and the NFIP elevated flag corroborates almost none of the remaining ICC elevations (0.7
percent flip after the payment). ZIP-level HMA acquisitions, the NPR address-level buyout
list matched through ATTOM, and the ATTOM record after the ICC year (improvement value,
transfers) do not separate the screened homes from the rest, because the NFIP-ATTOM link is a
cell-level proxy rather than an address match. ICC-sourced elevations are therefore reported
as uncorroborated, and the event studies use Builty permits only.

Sources: FEMA, Increased Cost of Compliance Coverage (fema.gov/floodplain-management/financial-help/increased-cost-compliance);
FEMA Fact Sheet, Increased Cost of Compliance Coverage (2020 posting); FEMA WYO Bulletin W-15038
(2015, four- to six-year completion window); Kousky and Lingle, "Post-Flood Mitigation: The NFIP's
Increased Cost of Compliance (ICC) Coverage", Wharton Risk Center Issue Brief, Fall 2017.
